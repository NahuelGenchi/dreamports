# Nintendo DS kit in C

[Back to the kits in C](../../c/README.md) · [Other consoles](../../../docs/CONSOLES.md#the-nintendo-ds) · [The Dreamport standard](../../../docs/STANDARD.md)

This folder is the Nintendo DS's kit for games written in C, on what [every console's kit in C](../../c/README.md) shares. It's the console's two processors' programs running side by side by the console's clock, its main memory, its two screens' display, its buttons, its touch screen and its sound, written from how [melonDS](https://github.com/melonDS-emu/melonDS), the tools' reference emulator, has them.

Like the rest of the kits it's under [GPL-3.0-or-later](../LICENSE), and nothing from any game is in it. melonDS is under the same licence, and its sound's tables and the order of its sums are followed here as melonDS has them.

## What's in it

| File | What it does |
| :--- | :--- |
| `nds.h` | What a game sees of the console: its two programs, the main memory, each processor's reads and writes of the hardware, and time |
| `nds.c` | The two programs side by side, the main memory, the screens, the buttons, the serial bus to the touch screen's chip, and what the page calls |
| `video.c` | The video memory's nine banks, the colours, the sprites' list, and the 2D engines' registers, text backgrounds and sprites |
| `ipc.c` | The shared memory, the ARM7's own, and the two processors' sync register and message queue |
| `irq.c` | Each processor's interrupt registers, display status and four timers |
| `dma.c` | Each processor's four DMA channels and the ARM9's fill registers |
| `math.c` | The ARM9's divider and square root |
| `cart.c` | The cartridge slot and the cartridge in it, read from the player's copy |
| `sound.c` | The sound: sixteen channels of 8-bit, 16-bit and ADPCM samples, square waves and noise, and the mixer, with its bias and its cut to 10 bits |
| `nds-kit.h` | What the kit's own files share, which a game doesn't need |

## A game in C

A game includes `"nds.h"` and defines its two programs, as its cartridge has one for each processor: `game_arm9()`, most of a game, and `game_arm7()`, which reads the touch screen and plays the sound. Each is written as its original runs. It never comes back, and waits for the screen with `nds9_wait_vblank` or `nds7_wait_vblank` where the original waits for the V-blank, giving how long after it starts the original's loop sees it.

Each reads and writes the hardware and the memory the two share through its own processor's functions, `nds9_read16`, `nds7_write32` and the rest, as the original's loads and stores do. Each has a time of its own, in ticks of the console's 33,513,982 Hz clock, which `nds9_spend` and `nds7_spend` move on by the cycles the original's code takes between two reads or writes, the ARM9's two to a tick. Before either reads or writes, the one that's behind runs first, up to the other's time, so a value one writes is there for the other's read only when the console's would be. The kit doesn't time a game's code itself: it's the game that says how long its original takes, as the NES's kit asks with `nes_spend`, and the proof tools check it on every frame.

A game whose original takes interrupts gives the kit its handler for each processor with `nds9_on_interrupt` and `nds7_on_interrupt`, in place of the address the original keeps at the end of the ARM9's data memory or of the ARM7's own, and waits for one as the original does: with `nds9_intr_wait` and `nds7_intr_wait` for the BIOS's IntrWait and VBlankIntrWait, `nds9_halt` for the ARM9's halt, and a write of `0x80` to `NDS_HALTCNT` for the ARM7's. The handler does its work through the processor's reads and writes, and says what it took in IF and in the BIOS's word before the address, as the original's does.

A frame of the proof tools runs from one V-blank's start to the next's, as what a frame shows and plays follows from what the programs wrote in the V-blank before it ([The Nintendo DS](../../../docs/CONSOLES.md#the-nintendo-ds)). The two programs start at tick 0, as the reference emulator starts a game's programs directly, and run until each first waits.

`npm run dp -- new <game>/c` on a Nintendo DS game writes the starting point on this kit, from `tools/libretro/new-game/c/nds.c.template`: both screens blue, and green while A is held.

## What it does

**The display.** Each engine, A and B, is on the screen the power register's top bit puts it on, as melonDS draws them: white when its display is off or blanked, black for A and white for B when the power register turns the engine off, and both screens black when the screens are off. With its display on, an engine draws mode 0, as its registers and memory hold it once the V-blank's writes are done: its backdrop, the first of its colours, then its four text backgrounds by their priority, from 3 to 0 and at the same priority from the fourth to the first, each of 16- or 256-colour tiles from its tile and map bases, at its scroll, 256 or 512 dots across and down, a tile flipped either way and, of 16 colours, in the bank of colours its map says.

**Sprites.** Each engine's 128, from its sprites' list at `0x07000000` or `0x07000400`, as melonDS draws them: of 16 or 256 colours, in each of their twelve sizes, flipped either way, their tiles mapped one way (1D), with its boundary, or the other (2D), at their place, wrapping round off each edge, each in front of the backgrounds of its priority and behind those of a lower one, an earlier sprite drawn over a later one at the same place unless the later has a lower priority. Their colours are the second 256 of each engine's, and their tiles in the banks given to that engine's sprites.

**The video memory.** Its nine banks, A to I, each given to a use by its control register, as melonDS's `MapVRAM` functions give them: at its own place (LCDC), at `0x06800000` and on, or to engine A's backgrounds, banks A to D at any of four places, E, and F and G at any of four 16 KB pages, or to engine B's, C, and H and I with their mirrors. Or to engine A's sprites, A and B at either of two places, E, and F and G, or to engine B's, D, and I with its mirrors. Each 16 KB page of an engine's backgrounds or sprites reads every bank given to it there, ORed, as the console's wiring does, and a write there goes to each of them. The ARM9's byte writes to the video memory, the colours and the sprites' list are lost, as the console's are. The display's status gives the V-blank's flag from line 192 to 261 and the H-blank's from 1,584 ticks into a line, with the line in the register after it.

**The memories.** The main memory, 4 MB at `0x02000000`, seen again every 4 MB. The ARM9's two fast memories where the console's firmware leaves them for a game it starts, as melonDS's direct start puts them: its code memory (ITCM), 32 KB, seen again and again up to `0x01FFFFFF`, and its data memory (DTCM), 16 KB at `0x03000000`, in front of the shared memory there, so the ARM9's writes there never reach it. The shared memory, 32 KB at `0x03000000`, which its control register gives all to the ARM9, all to the ARM7, or a half to each, as the firmware leaves it all the ARM7's; and the ARM7's own 64 KB, at `0x03800000` and, where it has none of the shared memory, at `0x03000000` too.

**The two processors talking.** Their sync register, four bits each way, and their message queue, 16 words each way, with its flags, its error when a processor sends to a full queue or takes from an empty one, and its clearing, as melonDS has them: taking from an empty queue gives the word in the place it would take from next, an old one or 0.

**The interrupts and the timers.** Each processor's IME, IE and IF, which a write of a 1 clears, and its display status's bits, as melonDS has them: the V-blank's interrupt, raised as line 192 starts where the status asks for it, the line it matches and its flag; each processor's four timers, counting the clock, a 64th, a 256th or a 1024th of it, or the timer before's overflows, reloading as each overflows and raising its interrupt; and the sync register's interrupt, asked for by the other's write, and the queue's, as a processor's own queue empties and as a word comes to the other's. A processor takes an interrupt as it next reads or writes once its time has reached it, and where it waits, for the V-blank or halted, at the interrupt's own time; the other processor runs up to it, where it can ask for one. The kit runs the BIOS's way to the game's handler and back, and its IntrWait, as melonDS's free BIOS does, which the proof tools start every game with: halted until an interrupt the processor has on comes, the ARM9 only with IME on, then the handler, then the BIOS's check of its word, with IME off meanwhile, until the handler has said it took one of those waited for.

**DMA.** Each processor's four channels, as melonDS has them: each one's source, destination and control, by halfwords or words, counting up, down or not at all, or up from where it started each time, started at once or at each V-blank's start, repeating, and with its interrupt at the end; and the ARM9's four fill registers, which a channel reads as any other place. A channel copies a unit at a time on its processor's bus, so the ARM9's never reaches its fast memories, each unit taking the time melonDS gives it from where it reads and writes, with the main memory's bursts from melonDS's own tables, while its processor waits. One started by a write runs then; one that waits for the V-blank runs from the V-blank's start, and the processor's own work after it waits as long.

**The divider and the square root.** The ARM9's, as melonDS has them: the divider's three sizes, 32 by 32, 64 by 32 and 64 by 64 bits, with its answer for a division by zero and its flag, and the square root of 32 or 64 bits. A halfword or word written to a control, or a word to a number, starts one again, and its result comes 18 ticks later for a 32-bit division, 34 for the others and 13 for a square root, worked out from the numbers as they are then, with the busy flag up and the last results there until then. So a game has to keep the time between its writes, as its original's instructions take it: a 32-bit division one write starts may be done before the next.

**The cartridge.** The slot and the cartridge in it, as melonDS has them once a game has been started directly, the cartridge already taking its last way of commands, whose encryption melonDS leaves out. Each processor has its own side of the slot, its control, its command and its two words of data, and the one EXMEMCNT gives the slot to, the ARM7 at first, talks to the cartridge: B7 reads 4 bytes at a time of the player's copy, round within a page of 0x1000 bytes, and from 0x8000 on for a place below it, and B8 gives the chip's number, as melonDS makes it from the copy's size. A transfer takes the time melonDS gives it, by the slot's speed and its two gaps, a word coming every 4 bytes' time while there's room for it, and ends as its last word is read, with its interrupt.

**The buttons and the touch screen.** Both processors read the ten buttons, and the ARM7 X, Y and the pen, at `0x04000136`. The ARM7 asks the touch screen's chip over the serial bus, a byte at a time, each transfer taking as long as its speed says, and the chip measures the pixel the page was touched at, as the pixel times 16.

**The sound.** Sixteen channels, each 8-bit, 16-bit or ADPCM samples from the main memory, read ahead 32 bytes at a time as the console's sound DMA does, and channels 8 to 13 a square wave of any of its 8 duties and 14 and 15 noise, each with its volume, divider and place between left and right. The mixer adds them up every 1,024 ticks from tick 1,024 on, takes the master volume, adds the bias, and keeps 10 bits of each sample, as the console's speakers get. The ARM7's power register mutes it.

## Not yet

The kit stops the game, so it never shows or plays what the console wouldn't, where a game turns on what isn't here yet: the background modes but 0, sprites that turn, blend, make a window, are bitmaps or use mosaic, windows, mosaic, blending, extended palettes, 3D, the master brightness, the banks' uses for textures, extended palettes and the ARM7, a display of the video memory or of the main memory, the sound's capture units and the mixer's other ways of taking channels 1 and 3, and a write to anything else of the hardware. The H-blank's and the line count's interrupts, the serial bus's, a word written to the display's status, which writes the line count, and the ARM7's halfword written to IF, which melonDS takes no notice of, stop a game too. Not there at all yet: moving the ARM9's fast memories, a processor's interrupts turned off in its own status (CPSR), as the kit takes them with only IME, IE and IF, DMA started by the H-blank, the display, the cartridge or the 3D engine, a transfer that writes to the cartridge, the save memory, or from or to anywhere else, which stops a game, the real-time clock, the BIOS's other calls, the microphone and the lid. The BIOS's own time, on the way to a handler and back and in and out of a call, is the kit's reckoning, which no test has measured on its own yet: the test below measures what the original's start and the BIOS's way take together. Each comes as a game needs it, proven against melonDS with a program of the project's own.

## How it's checked

`npm run test:real-nds` proves a Dreamport in C of a small Nintendo DS program of the project's own on this kit ([`tools/test/made-up-nds.c`](../../../tools/test/made-up-nds.c)), its two programs written again, against melonDS: every frame of both screens and the sound, with buttons and touches from a recording, and finds one that reads a touch in the wrong place. `npm run test:nds-kit` proves another, a program that gives banks A and C to the two engines' backgrounds and scrolls 16- and 256-colour tiles at two priorities, flipped every way ([`tools/test/made-up-nds-bg.mjs`](../../../tools/test/made-up-nds-bg.mjs)), on every frame, and finds a Dreamport that draws a map unflipped or a background behind where it belongs; then a program with sprites on both engines, of 16 and 256 colours, flipped, mapped both ways and coming in off the edges among a background ([`tools/test/made-up-nds-obj.mjs`](../../../tools/test/made-up-nds-obj.mjs)), and finds a sprite left unflipped or put in front of the background it's behind; then a program whose two processors talk through the queue, the sync register and the shared memory, with the ARM9's data memory over it ([`tools/test/made-up-nds-ipc.mjs`](../../../tools/test/made-up-nds-ipc.mjs)), and finds a Dreamport that takes the wrong half of the shared memory; then a program whose two processors wait for the V-blank in the BIOS, count their timers' and each other's interrupts in their handlers and read a timer that counts the clock there, shown to the tick on every other frame ([`tools/test/made-up-nds-irq.mjs`](../../../tools/test/made-up-nds-irq.mjs)), and finds a Dreamport whose timer counts four times as fast. Its Dreamport keeps the time the original's start and its way from the V-blank to a timer's control take, as the test's maker read them on melonDS from the timers. Then a program whose processors copy and fill with DMA, at once and at each V-blank, by halfwords and words, up, down and from a fill register, and whose ARM9 times a copy each frame, four ways in turn, 20 to 147 units, shown to the tick on every other frame ([`tools/test/made-up-nds-dma.mjs`](../../../tools/test/made-up-nds-dma.mjs)), and finds a Dreamport whose copy counts up where it counts down. Then a program that divides and takes square roots of each frame's numbers, each size, by zero among them, reading the answer too soon and once it's there ([`tools/test/made-up-nds-math.mjs`](../../../tools/test/made-up-nds-math.mjs)), whose Dreamport keeps the original's instructions between its writes, 7 ticks each as the test's maker read them on melonDS, and finds a Dreamport that leaves out a numerator's high word. Then a program whose ARM9 takes the slot and reads its own cartridge four ways in turn, 0x200 bytes round a page's end, 4 bytes from a place that isn't a word's, the chip's number and 0x200 bytes from below 0x8000, at both speeds, counting each transfer's end by its interrupt's flag ([`tools/test/made-up-nds-cart.mjs`](../../../tools/test/made-up-nds-cart.mjs)), and finds a Dreamport that writes a command's place lowest byte first. Its transfers' time isn't shown: on melonDS the program's own loop, waiting for each word, decides it.
